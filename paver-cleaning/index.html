More than pressure washing

Professional paver cleaning starts with identifying what is actually on the surface.

A dirty paver driveway can contain algae, mildew, traffic film, tire residue, irrigation minerals, rust, oil, leaf tannins, loose joint material, and remnants of old sealer at the same time. Those are different problems. A professional paver cleaning company should not treat every stain as if more pressure is the answer.

Paver pressure washing done correctly

Pressure washing is one of the tools used to clean concrete and brick pavers, but the cleaning result comes from the full process: identifying the surface, selecting appropriate chemistry when needed, allowing enough dwell time, using controlled mechanical cleaning, and rinsing thoroughly.

On porous pavers, an overly tight nozzle pattern or excessive pressure can expose aggregate, leave wand marks, disturb stable coatings, or blow joint sand out faster than necessary. On previously sealed pavers, aggressive cleaning can also create patchy coating damage that looks worse once the surface dries.

For homeowners comparing pressure washing in Jacksonville, FL, the important question is not simply how much PSI a machine produces. It is whether the contractor understands pavers as a system of surface, joints, existing sealer, drainage, and surrounding materials.

What we evaluate

Different paver stains need different cleaning strategies.

Algae, mildew & organic growth

Florida humidity, shade, irrigation, and slow-drying areas can support green or dark organic buildup. Appropriate cleaning chemistry and dwell time can reduce the need for unnecessarily aggressive pressure.

Oil, grease & vehicle residue

Fresh surface contamination may respond to specialty degreasing. Older oil can migrate into porous concrete pavers and leave a residual shadow even after professional treatment.

Rust & irrigation staining

Orange irrigation or metal staining usually calls for dedicated chemistry rather than stronger pressure. Stain age and the surface material affect how much improvement is possible.

Leaf tannins & dark spots

Organic staining from leaves, landscaping, and standing debris can bond differently than ordinary dirt. Treatment is selected after the surface and stain are evaluated.

Efflorescence & mineral deposits

White mineral haze is not the same as mildew or soil. Efflorescence and mineral deposits may need specialty restoration chemistry and should not be attacked by simply increasing pressure.

Failed or cloudy sealer

Whitening, peeling, trapped moisture, or heavy film buildup can be a coating problem rather than a cleaning problem. Those surfaces may need resealing preparation or stripping instead of ordinary power washing.

Stain-removal expectations matter.

Pavers and natural stone are porous. Professional cleaning can dramatically improve many stains, but complete removal cannot be promised when oil, rust, minerals, tannins, or previous coatings have penetrated deeply or chemically changed the surface.

Soft wash vs. pressure wash

“Soft washing” and “pressure washing” are methods—not one-size-fits-all answers.

When chemistry should do more of the work

Organic growth often responds better when the correct cleaner is applied and given time to work. This can reduce the amount of mechanical force needed. That is the principle behind soft-wash cleaning: use chemistry and controlled application instead of relying only on pressure.

But paver cleaning can still require mechanical cleaning and careful rinsing, especially on driveways with traffic film or surfaces being prepared for sealing. Calling every project a “soft wash” can be just as misleading as assuming every project should be blasted at full pressure.

Why joints change the cleaning plan

Joint sand is part of the paver system. If joints are already low, loose, or eroded, high-pressure cleaning can accelerate sand loss. The condition of the joints should be recognized before cleaning starts.

If a cleaning-only project reveals low joints, that does not automatically mean every surface must be resanded. If cleaning is part of a full sealing project, however, joint restoration may become part of the preparation scope before sealer is applied.

Cleaning by surface area

Paver cleaning around a Florida home changes with how the area is used.

Driveway paver cleaning

Driveways commonly carry tire marks, traffic film, oil residue, irrigation staining, sun exposure, and heavier joint wear. Driveway pressure washing should clean the surface without needlessly scarring pavers or excavating the joints.

Pool deck paver cleaning

Pool decks add screened enclosures, coping, drains, splash zones, wet walking areas, and pool-water concerns. Overspray and runoff control matter as much as cleaning strength.

Patio & walkway cleaning

Shaded patios and side walkways often hold organic growth longer than open driveways. Chemistry, drainage, landscaping, and adjacent walls or doors all affect the cleaning setup.

Travertine & natural stone

Natural stone is not treated like ordinary concrete pavers. Stone-safe chemistry and lower-impact technique are selected around porosity, fill, surface finish, and existing protection.

Cleaning before sealing

Sealer should never be used to hide a dirty or contaminated surface.

Preparation determines the finish

When paver cleaning is the first stage of a sealing project, the goal is not simply to make the pavers look cleaner while they are wet. Dirt, active organic growth, cleaner residue, loose material, and incompatible coating problems need to be addressed before a new sealer system is considered.

After cleaning, the surface needs adequate rinsing and drying. Existing coating compatibility, moisture, joint condition, weather, and the desired finish are then evaluated separately. Cleaning and sealing are connected steps, but they are not the same step.

When washing is not enough

Some “dirty pavers” are really restoration problems.

A reputable pressure washing company should know when to stop washing and explain the underlying condition. More pressure cannot correct every surface defect.

Peeling or whitening sealer

If the coating is failing, patchy, cloudy, or moisture-damaged, the project may need resealing preparation or stripping rather than another cleaning pass.

Sunken or loose pavers

Cleaning does not rebuild a failed base or level pavers. Selected small reset and re-level work should be addressed separately before the finished restoration.

Deep mineral or chemical staining

Efflorescence, irrigation minerals, rust, and deeply absorbed oil may need specialty treatment and realistic expectations about residual discoloration.

Paver cleaning FAQ

Common questions about pressure washing pavers

Can pressure washing damage pavers?

Yes. Excessive pressure, cleaning too close to the surface, or poor technique can etch softer pavers, expose aggregate, disturb coatings, and remove joint sand. The goal is controlled cleaning, not maximum pressure.

Is soft washing better than pressure washing for pavers?

It depends on the material and contamination. Organic buildup can often be loosened with appropriate chemistry and dwell time, reducing the amount of mechanical force required. Driveways and other paver surfaces may still need controlled mechanical cleaning and rinsing.

Can you remove algae and mildew from pavers?

Most organic buildup can be substantially improved when the right chemistry, dwell time, cleaning technique, and rinsing are used. Shade, drainage, porosity, and how long the growth has been present affect the final result.

Will pressure washing remove joint sand?

Aggressive washing can. We inspect the joints first and use controlled technique to reduce unnecessary sand loss. If joints are already low or unstable, we explain that before cleaning.

Do I have to seal my pavers after cleaning?

No. Cleaning can be a standalone service. If sealing is planned, the cleaning stage becomes preparation for the coating and is followed by drying, joint evaluation, and sealer-compatibility checks.

Can normal pressure washing fix white or cloudy pavers?

Not always. White mineral deposits, efflorescence, trapped moisture, or failed sealer are different conditions. They may require specialty chemistry, resealing preparation, or stripping rather than ordinary cleaning.
