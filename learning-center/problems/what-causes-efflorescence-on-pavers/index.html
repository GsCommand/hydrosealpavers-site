What Causes Efflorescence on Pavers?

Efflorescence is the white mineral residue left when moisture moves through concrete or masonry, carries dissolved salts to the surface, and evaporates.

Updated July 30, 2026 · HydroSeal field guidance

What efflorescence is

Efflorescence is not mold and is not automatically a sealer defect. It usually appears as a powdery white haze, streak, or deposit on concrete pavers, brick, walls, or coping. New pavers can release salts from manufacturing and installation materials. Older surfaces can develop deposits when irrigation, rain, groundwater, or drainage repeatedly moves moisture through the system.

Why Florida sees so much of it

Frequent rain, humidity, irrigation, high water tables, and wet-dry cycling provide the moisture movement needed for salts to migrate. Low areas, wall bases, driveway bottoms, and shaded zones often show the strongest deposits.

How to distinguish it from sealer whitening

Dry powder that can be reduced by brushing suggests efflorescence. Milky film, peeling, or haze trapped beneath a coating suggests sealer failure. Both problems can occur together, so diagnosis should happen before treatment.

Treatment and limitations

Use a product designed for efflorescence on the specific surface, follow dilution and dwell instructions, agitate when required, and rinse thoroughly. Multiple treatments may be needed. Treatment removes current deposits but cannot guarantee salts will never return. Correcting drainage and moisture sources reduces recurrence.

Frequently asked questions

Will pressure washing remove efflorescence?

It may remove loose powder but often does not dissolve bonded mineral residue.

Can it return after sealing?

Yes. Sealing can reduce water entry from above but cannot eliminate every source below.

Should new pavers be treated before sealing?

If deposits are present, they should be evaluated and removed before coating.
